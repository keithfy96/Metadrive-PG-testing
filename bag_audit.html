Did the Phase 5 bag lose messages?

No. Across 1,466,940 messages and 7.41 GB, 39 of 42 periodic channels recorded within 0.1% of nominal and the writer never once fell behind. One channel is short of its configured rate — and it was short before the recorder ever saw it. Two drives from the ROS 1 archive, read the same way, say the same thing about the stack this one replaced.

The bag is complete and usable

All six rig cameras landed 15,589–15,590 frames — a spread of one. The GNSS/INS stack, the 100 Hz vehicle bus and the 10 Hz perception chain are all exactly on rate. Reads as loss, isn't

Three things that will look wrong on a first pass

bursty

/sensing/gnss/imu/velocity — 76% of intervals under 0.5 ms

The SBG driver delivers in bursts of four followed by a ~20 ms gap. The mean interval is exactly 5.00 ms: a clean 200 Hz. A naive median-interval check calls this 99% loss.

aperiodic

/tf, /rosout, /diagnostics have no nominal rate

Multi-publisher and event-driven. /tf at 86.6 Hz is the sum of several broadcasters, not a 100 Hz topic that lost 13%.

sensor clock

/sensing/lidar/imu at 202.92 Hz, not 200

The Livox IMU free-runs slightly fast. 200 Hz is the datasheet figure, not a rate it fell short of.

Where the message count goes

ROS 1 wrote 3.7× the messages at the same bytes per second

The ROS 1 stack fanned every decoded CAN signal out to its own topic, each carrying one scalar in a geometry_msgs/TwistStamped — a header plus six float64s to hold a single number. 95 of its 132 topics are one CAN signal, and 104 of its 137 connections are that message type. It is why the older bag averages 7,042 messages a second against this one's 1,882, and 1,799 bytes a message against 7,159. The byte rates barely differ — 12.7 MB/s against 13.5 — so what the fan-out cost was never disk. It was 3.7× the per-message overhead, 132 topics to reason about instead of 55, and a decode spread across a hundred topics that have to be re-joined to mean anything.

A loss test ROS 1 hands you

The CAN counters look like 123 missing frames. They aren't.

Six of those per-signal topics are the rolling COUNTER of a CAN message — a wire sequence number sitting in a topic of its own, which makes a direct drop test almost free. Read across 101,327 counter messages, it finds 19 holes and 123 frames unaccounted for. That reads like loss.

It isn't. Every hole was checked against the arrival gap that should accompany it: a genuinely missing frame leaves a gap of 40 ms or more at 50 Hz. Not one does. The gaps at those holes run 5.9–19.5 ms — shorter than the nominal 20 ms period. Messages arrived early with the counter jumping, and 17 of the 19 land in a single 0.1 s burst at t=10.8 s. The other drive reproduces it at t=10.6 s. It is the ADAS transmit path settling after startup, not a recorder dropping frames.

What each format can prove

The two bags answer different questions about loss

MCAP only

The writer-latency proof does not exist for a ROS 1 bag

rosbag v2.0 stores one timestamp per message — when the recorder received it. MCAP stores that and the DDS source timestamp, so publish → disk is a measurable quantity. On the ROS 1 side, "was the recorder keeping up" has no answer in the file; you are left with counts against nominal.

easier in ROS 1

The wire counters sit in their own topics

A by-product of the per-signal fan-out: a rolling CAN counter is a scalar topic you can read without a DBC. The same counters exist in this bag, inside the frames on /vehicle/can_rx — the test is still available, it just needs a decode first.

both bags

Neither format flags a dropped message

Nothing in either file says "a message was here and is gone". Every loss figure on this page is inferred — from rates against nominal, from gaps in time, from sequence holes. That is why a finding needs two of them to agree before it counts.

Unchanged by the migration

The sensor layer records at exactly the same rates

Same hardware, two stacks, three drives across four weeks. Every shared sensor stream lands within 0.08 Hz. The Livox IMU's 202.9 Hz — the one figure on this page that sits off its datasheet number — is 202.88, 202.92 and 202.96 across the three, which settles it as the sensor's own clock rather than anything either recorder did.

Six streams, 10 Hz, synchronised to 0.000 ms

Every camera loses frames exactly once, and all of it inside the first half second: 3–7 frames each while the encoders start. cam3's 795 ms gap is the worst and is why it finishes three frames short. After t = 0.5 s not one frame is missing from any stream.

the trade

10 Hz out-of-band against 20 Hz in-band

The ROS 1 rig wrote 18,424 frames in 0.91 GB of HEVC over 307 s. The ROS 2 rig wrote 93,535 frames in 0.67 GB of ffmpeg over 780 s — twice the rate, inside the bag, indexed alongside everything else. What ROS 1 bought for that was a codec the recorder never had to touch; what it cost is that the pixels and their metadata live in different files, joined only by a timestamp.

How this was measured

The host has neither the mcap Python module nor a ROS 2 CLI, so the file was read directly.

  1. Parse the MCAP summary section for channels, chunk indexes and statistics, then read every MessageIndex record — this yields all 1,466,940 log timestamps without decompressing a byte of payload (0.3 s for the whole 7.41 GB file).
  2. Derive per-topic rate, interval histograms and gap counts from those timestamps.
  3. For latency, decompress a 10% chunk sample (216 of 2,165 chunks) and read each Message record's publish_time — the DDS source timestamp — against its log_time.
  4. Decode /rosout from the same sample for driver counters and the shutdown sequence.
  5. For the ROS 1 bags, the same approach against rosbag v2.0: the bag header's index_pos, then the connection and chunk-info records, then each chunk's trailing IndexData records for every message's time and offset. Those chunks are uncompressed, so the six COUNTER topics could be read back by seeking straight to their offsets — 101,327 messages without touching the other 2.3 million.
  6. Per-topic byte totals in both formats come from differencing consecutive message offsets within a chunk against the chunk's uncompressed size — exact, and again without reading a payload.

Nominal rates are inferred by snapping each measured rate to the nearest standard rate; /localization/odometry's target was confirmed against rl_odom_fusion.yaml.
